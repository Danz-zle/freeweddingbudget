A wedding timeline should show the financial effect of each decision. Record when a quote becomes a contract, whether a deposit is refundable, when the guest count changes an invoice, and which final balances are due together.

The 12-month outline is an example, not a booking rule. Shorten or extend each phase for a six-month wedding, courthouse ceremony, destination event, or multi-day celebration. Adjust it for local requirements, vendor availability, mailing time, weather, and customs.

Five things to keep under control

  1. Scope. Date range, guest count, event type, location, and priorities.
  2. Budget. Total ceiling, category limits, reserve, and confirmed contributions.
  3. Contracts. Comparable scope, full total, exclusions, deposit, and cancellation terms.
  4. Cash flow. Every due date, remaining balance, and month-by-month funding requirement.
  5. Change control. A clear point after which guest additions, upgrades, or timeline changes require a budget trade-off.

12 to 10 months before: decide what you are planning

Set a safe spending ceiling and a working guest-count range. Decide which two or three priorities matter most, then check what kind of event the budget can support. You can research venues and major vendors now, but headline prices are not useful until you compare a similar scope.

6 to 4 months before: work out how the day will run

Now connect each vendor's obligations to the event schedule. Find out who needs meals, power, loading access, parking, accommodation, rehearsal time, or help with setup. Allow enough time for attire fittings and alterations, and track their expected total cost rather than only the purchase price.

Mid-planning audit

  • Replace rough estimates with current written quotes.
  • Check whether booked categories still fit the total ceiling.
  • List all remaining balances by month, not only by vendor.
  • Confirm venue access, loading, power, insurance, permits, and cleanup responsibilities.
  • Create a first weather or emergency backup decision process.
  • Pause optional upgrades until the future-payment calendar is covered.

3 to 2 months before: update costs tied to the guest list

As replies arrive, update catering, rentals, stationery, transportation, seating, accessibility support, and the venue layout. Ask each vendor when final information is due and when a lower guest count will stop reducing the invoice.

Final month and final week: check every remaining balance

Several large balances can land in the final month. Check the payment calendar, payment method, invoice recipient, contract total, and proof of earlier payments before they are due. This is also the time to confirm arrival times, contact numbers, setup and cleanup responsibilities, transportation, guest assistance, and the weather decision process.

  • Match every final invoice against the signed scope and approved changes.
  • Confirm the remaining balance rather than assuming the vendor subtracted deposits correctly.
  • Keep receipts and payment confirmations in one shared location.
  • Assign one person to each vendor arrival, setup area, and final handoff.
  • Use official local weather and emergency alerts for outdoor or travel-sensitive events.
  • Do not spend the remaining reserve on non-essential additions until all known balances are covered.

Put every vendor payment on the calendar

  1. Start in Budget with the ceiling and category limits; test the likely attendance range in Guests.
  2. Compare complete quotes in Vendors before choosing. Select only the quote you intend to treat as committed.
  3. Enter every contract due date in Payments: deposit, installments, and final balance.
  4. Use Scheduled, Due Soon, Overdue, and Paid to review cash flow. A payment marked paid is not added again to vendor cost.
  5. Revisit guest scenarios at RSVP and vendor guarantee deadlines, then review Budget before approving optional changes.
  6. Export the payment schedule from Reports when you need an offline checklist.

FAQ

Do I need exactly 12 months to plan a wedding?

No. The order matters more than the number of months. Start with the event scope and budget, compare complete quotes, sign clear contracts, and put every payment on the calendar. Update costs when the guest count changes and be cautious about additions near the end.

What should I record when paying a deposit?

Record the full contract total, deposit amount, payment date, remaining balance, every future due date, refund terms, and the conditions that may change the price.

When should I stop adding upgrades?

Stop when future balances, hidden costs, and the reserve are not fully covered. An available bank balance today is not the same as money available after all signed commitments are paid.

When should I check marriage-license requirements?

Check early enough to understand the documents, process, validity window, waiting period, witness rules, and filing steps, then verify again close enough to the ceremony to follow the correct local timing.
