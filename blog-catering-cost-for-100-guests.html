Catering costs

Wedding catering for 100 guests: what a $100 menu price becomes

DL
Written by Dan Liu
Published November 17, 2026

A catering price per guest is only the start of the bill. In five published 2026 price lists from New York, Arizona, Florida and Wisconsin, the percentage charges added after the menu price run from 15% to 28%, and sales tax follows. Applied to a round $100 per guest and 100 guests, a $10,000 menu becomes between $12,300 and $13,926.40, before drinks, rentals or anything else. This guide shows the arithmetic venue by venue so you can run the same sum on your own quote.

The short answer

  • Multiply the per-guest price by your guest count, then add the percentage charge to that subtotal. Add sales tax after it, and check whether the venue taxes the percentage charge as well as the food.
  • The size of the percentage matters more than most menu choices. On a $10,000 menu, the gap between a 15% fee and a 28% charge is $1,300 before any tax.
  • The $100 figure here is a round number for the sums. It is not any venue's price, and nothing in this guide says what a couple paid.

Five price lists, one sum

Each of these documents prints the percentage charges that sit on top of its menu prices. The table applies them to the same illustration: 100 guests at $100 each, a $10,000 menu. Where a document says the charge is taxed, the tax is applied to the menu plus the charge. Where it does not say, both readings are shown.

Why the taxed charge matters

Three of the five documents state that the service charge is itself subject to sales tax, and two do not say either way. The difference is real money. On the Howey Mansion's printed terms, a $10,000 menu is $13,000.00 if the 7% tax applies to the food only, and $13,161.00 if it also applies to the 23% charge, a $161 gap. At Swan Dolphin's 26% and 6.5% the gap is $169. At 100 guests the amounts are small next to the total, but they are the kind of line that appears for the first time on an invoice.

The words used for the percentage also differ. Marlow Events calls its 15% an administrative fee that "is not a gratuity" and says gratuities are appreciated but not expected. The other four call theirs a service charge. Whether any of it reaches the staff is a question for the venue, not something the label settles. Ask what the percentage covers, whether a separate tip is expected, and whether tax is added to it.

Documents can also disagree with themselves. El Chorro's 2027 venue document prints "28% taxable service charge" in its price footer and, in its terms, "A 27% taxable service charge". A one-point difference on a $10,000 menu is about $100 before tax. If two figures appear, ask which one your contract will use and get it in writing.

Not all 100 guests may pay the adult price

A guest count is not always a headcount of adults. Monona Terrace's 2026 menu lists its Premium package at $84.95 per adult and $24.95 per child. For 100 adults that is $8,495.00, and the 23% charge adds $1,953.85, for $10,448.85 before sales tax. If 10 of the 100 guests are children, the food is 90 adults at $84.95 ($7,645.50) plus 10 children at $24.95 ($249.50), a subtotal of $7,895.00. With the 23% charge that is $9,710.85, or $738.00 less than the all-adult figure, before tax.

Marlow Events prints a similar split: children aged 5 and under are free, ages 6 to 12 are $50 for food and beverage, and ages 13 to 20 pay a full-priced meal with a reduced non-alcoholic beverage fee. It also lists vendor meals at $40 per person. Six vendor meals add $240 before any fee or tax. Count the children, the vendors and any other people who need a meal as separate lines, not as part of one round number.

What the total still leaves out

These totals use one number, the menu price per guest. Real quotes add more lines, and the documents above name several of them. The Howey Mansion's packages are priced for 75 or more guests and say a food and beverage minimum is required. Marlow says every event is subject to a food and beverage minimum that depends on the date, and does not print the amount. Monona Terrace lists bartender fees, chef attendants and other labour charges, and says labour is also subject to sales tax.

  • Drinks. Bar packages, kegs and toasts are often separate from the food price, and the percentage charge usually applies to them too.
  • Minimums. If your guest count or spend falls below a minimum, the venue may bill the difference, so a smaller list does not always lower the bill.
  • Staffing and rentals. Bartenders, attendants, linens, extra place settings and décor setup can sit outside the per-guest price.
  • Validity. Several of these documents say prices can change. A published price is not a held price.

Run the same sum on your own quote

  1. Get the per-guest price in writing, and write down exactly what it includes: courses, non-alcoholic drinks, staff, rentals, cake cutting.
  2. Multiply by the guests who will eat, using separate lines for adults, children and vendors if the quote prices them differently.
  3. Add the percentage charge to that subtotal. Ask what it is called, what it covers, and whether a tip is separate.
  4. Add sales tax and ask whether it applies to the charge, the labour and the drinks as well as the food. If the rate is missing, mark the total as a minimum.
  5. Add the separate lines: bar, labour fees, rentals and any minimum. Compare totals, not menu prices.

As a formula: total = (guests × price + fixed fees) × (1 + percentage charge), then tax on whatever the quote says is taxable. Once you have a like-for-like total, enter it as a quote in the Planner's Vendors section so it sits beside the rest of your budget.

What these examples cannot tell you

They are published fee rules applied to an illustrative price, not quotes and not what any couple paid. The five documents are not a sample of the market, and they were chosen because each prints its percentage charges online. Documents change, and I found no date on the Swan Dolphin menu. For El Chorro, Howey Mansion and Swan Dolphin, this guide uses only the printed fee wording, not their per-guest prices. Taxes, tipping rules and contract terms vary by place, and this is general information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Confirm every figure with the venue before you rely on it.
