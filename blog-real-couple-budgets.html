Real budgets

What nine real wedding budgets show: totals that do not add up, and how far couples went over

DL
Written by Dan Liu
Published November 8, 2026

In nine itemized budgets published by Zola and Bridebook, only 2 had category lines that added up to the stated total. In 5 the lines fell short, by as much as $16,911, and in 2 they added up to more than the total. Of the 7 couples whose pages give an original budget, 5 spent more and 2 spent less. This guide checks the arithmetic and shows what it means for your own plan. It is a small, hand-picked sample, not an average.

Your starting checklist

  • Make your category lines add up to your total, with an explicit line for costs you have not itemized.
  • Write down what each venue or package price includes before you compare categories.
  • Keep your original budget next to your running total so you can see the gap.
  • Treat another couple's category figures as a story, not a benchmark.

Nine budgets, and whether the lines add up

Eight of these are Zola's "budget diary" pages, where couples shared their own figures for weddings in 2022. The ninth is a Bridebook "Wedding Spend" article about a May 2026 wedding in England, in pounds. I added up each page's listed categories myself and compared the result with the stated total. Zola's pages say individual figures may not total the overall budget because of approximations and omitted items, so a gap is expected, but its size is worth seeing.

Two budgets reconcile, one in California and one in Louisiana. Five list less than the total. The largest gap is $16,911 in the Indiana page, which is about 34% of that couple's stated total. Two list more than the total, Nebraska and Pennsylvania, so the lines overshoot the headline figure. The Nebraska page does not even price its wedding bands line, so its true listed sum would be higher still.

Scope explains part of this. In the New Hampshire page the venue price includes the food; in the Indiana page it includes catering and alcohol; in the Pennsylvania page catering, alcohol and music are not itemized separately. The Florida page adds a bachelor weekend and accommodations line of $5,370 that most budgets would not count. A category comparison between these pages is therefore unreliable even before the arithmetic gaps.

Six pages state a guest count. Dividing the stated total by it gives very different figures.

The spread runs from $50 to $1,000 per guest, a twentyfold difference. The $50 figure belongs to a 200-guest wedding with a $2,100 venue and $100 of flowers; the $1,000 figure belongs to a 90-guest wedding that includes a bachelor weekend and a wedding planner. Neither is a price you can expect, which is why this guide does not give a typical figure. If you want to see how guest count changes real vendor pricing, the 50 vs 100 guests guide works through it. The pound figure for England is not directly comparable with the dollar ones, and the two-currency guide explains how to convert.

Only two pages reconcile, so they are the only ones where category shares mean something. The table shows each line as a share of the stated total.

Food and drink are the largest line in both, at about a third to two fifths of the total, but the venue share differs by more than ten points. Two weddings are not a rule. They show that the same total can be spent in different shapes, so decide your own shape from your own quotes.

What to do with this in your own budget

  1. Make the lines add up. Give yourself an explicit "other and unallocated" line so your categories equal your total. Most gaps above are costs that were spent but not itemized.
  2. Record what is included. A venue that includes food is not comparable with one that does not. Note inclusions beside each price.
  3. Plan for a range, not a point. In this sample, couples moved by up to 100% from their original figure. That is not a forecast for you, but it is a reason to hold a reserve and to decide in advance what you would cut.
  4. Keep the original. Write your first budget down and compare it with your running total. Knowing the gap early is the point.

What these budgets cannot tell you

This is nine pages: eight from one American publisher's series about 2022 weddings, one from a British publisher about a 2026 wedding. I picked the pages that appeared in my searches, and I did not check the figures against receipts or contact any couple. The pages are self-reported and edited, the American ones were last updated between February 2024 and September 2025, and 2022 prices are not 2026 prices. This guide uses regions only and names no one. It is general information, not financial advice, and describes no personal experience.
